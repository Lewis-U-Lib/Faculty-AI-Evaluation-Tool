    🎯 Faculty Focus What is your primary goal right now?
    Choose this when the work in front of you is a course: planning it, running it, or helping students learn in it. Results lean toward classroom-facing ideas — activities, prompts, assessment scaffolds — rather than tools aimed at your own publication pipeline.
    Choose this when the work is your own scholarship: a paper, a dataset, a grant, a book chapter. Results lean toward discovery, synthesis, drafting, and project-management support, and away from student-facing classroom activities.
    Choose this when the same work serves teaching and research at once — a course built around your research area, students working on your project, or a methods class that doubles as a lab. Results blend both sets.
    Choose this for program-level work rather than a single course: assessment reporting, curriculum mapping, accreditation narratives, annual review. Results lean toward synthesis, documentation, and evidence-gathering across many courses.
    ⏱️ Time & Depth How much time or scope does this involve?
    A single class session or less, with almost no setup. Good when you want to try one AI-aware move without redesigning anything — results favor activities you could run this week.
    A one-to-two week stretch of systematic searching and sorting. Results favor discovery, screening, and synthesis support, and the checkpoints lean toward verification of what a tool surfaces.
    Two to four weeks of drafting and revising. Results favor feedback loops, structural revision, and ideas that make the thinking visible between drafts rather than only at the end.
    Four to eight weeks toward a high-stakes external submission. Results favor careful drafting support, compliance and formatting help, and stronger cautions about confidentiality and funder rules.
    A structured one-to-two week unit inside a larger course. Results favor sequences — a set of connected activities with a beginning and an end — rather than one-off exercises.
    A multi-week deliverable with feedback built into the middle. Results favor staged work, checkpoints, and ideas that survive contact with a revision cycle.
    A sustained, original, high-stakes project across a term. Results favor independence, process documentation, and integrity safeguards proportional to what is at stake.
    👥 Audience & Modality Who are your students and how do you meet?
    Students newly arrived at college-level work. Results assume more scaffolding, more explicit modeling, and more attention to building judgment before handing over a tool that can do the task for them.
    Students with a foundation in the field who are applying and analyzing. Results assume more independence and more disciplinary specificity than intro-level ideas.
    Students working toward original contribution. Results assume methodological seriousness, and lean toward research workflow, synthesis, and professional norms.
    Not students at all — peers, reviewers, editors, grant panels. Results favor tools and framings appropriate to professional scholarly communication rather than instruction.
    Fully asynchronous and self-paced. Results favor ideas that work without a live room: written artifacts, asynchronous discussion, and activities that do not depend on simultaneous presence.
    Mixed modality, where attendance and format vary between students. Results favor ideas that hold up whether a given student is in the room or not.
    🔭 Discipline What field or cluster best fits your course?
    Clinical reasoning, evidence-based practice, and simulation. Results lean toward patient-safety framing, source currency, and strong verification, since clinical claims carry direct risk.
    Data, modeling, lab work, and quantitative reasoning. Results lean toward computation, code, dataset handling, and checking a tool’s arithmetic rather than trusting it.
    Rhetorical, interpretive, and historical work. Results lean toward close reading, argument, and the difference between a text’s claims and a summary of them.
    Empirical and policy work across qualitative and quantitative methods. Results lean toward study design, coding, and interpretation of evidence.
    Cases, strategy, finance, and management. Results lean toward scenario work, analysis of real organizations, and professional communication.
    Pedagogy, assessment, and curriculum. Results lean toward instructional design and the meta-level question of what a tool does to learning.
    Information literacy, curation, metadata, and access. Results lean toward search behavior, evaluation of sources, and the systems that make things findable.
    Studio practice, performance, critique, and production. Results lean toward making, iteration, and questions of authorship and originality.
    Work that crosses fields rather than sitting in one. Results favor integrative framing and are less likely to assume one discipline’s conventions.
    ✏️ Teaching Task What specific job are you trying to do?
    Building or rebuilding a course or unit: outcomes, sequence, alignment. Results favor backward design and structural work rather than single activities.
    Getting students talking and keeping them engaged — prompts, facilitation moves, participation structures, in person or asynchronous.
    Designing what students produce and how it is judged: authentic tasks, rubrics, grading approaches, and assessment that is harder to shortcut.
    The loop between drafts — formative comments, peer review, and revision structures that make improvement visible.
    Finding, screening, and synthesizing sources, and identifying what is missing. Results emphasize verification, since fabricated citations are a known failure mode.
    Creating or adapting course content: readings, OER, slides, and accessibility of the materials themselves.
    Your own project pipeline — methods, data handling, writing, and grant work.
    Arts practice and creative production, where questions of authorship, process, and originality sit in the foreground.
    Interviews, coding, and thematic analysis. Results emphasize researcher judgment and the risk of a tool flattening nuance in the data.
    Faculty administrative work: accreditation, program review, reports, and the documentation that surrounds teaching rather than the teaching itself.
    Syllabus language, disclosure expectations, and academic integrity. Results favor clear articulation of what is and is not permitted, and why.
    🧠 Bloom's Level & Topic What cognitive depth? (Optional topic for context)
    Recall and basic comprehension — defining, describing, explaining. The level where an AI tool most easily substitutes for the thinking, so results emphasize verification and retrieval practice.
    Interpreting, classifying, summarizing, and inferring — going beyond recall to explaining in one’s own terms.
    Using knowledge in a new situation: executing a procedure, implementing a method, demonstrating a skill.
    Breaking something into parts and examining how they relate — differentiating, organizing, comparing, attributing.
    Judging against criteria — critiquing, justifying, arguing a position and defending it.
    Producing something new: designing, constructing, composing. The level where authorship questions matter most, so results emphasize process evidence.

    📚 Reference
    What do these filters and labels mean?

    Lower-stakes ideas are possible starting points that appear shorter, easier to try, or better suited to faculty-only exploration before anything is adapted for a course. This is a cautious-exploration cue, not a security, privacy, policy, accessibility, or disciplinary guarantee.

    Teaching/course ideas are faculty-facing possibilities for courses, assignments, discussions, assessment, or student-learning contexts. The tool is still written for faculty, not for students using it directly.

    Faculty workflow ideas emphasize faculty research, administrative work, planning, course design, materials development, or scholarly workflows.

    1 The Big Picture

    A Guided Carousel → Results Below

    The tool has three zones. Down the left sits a sticky column that changes as you scroll — step pills while you are choosing, result controls once you reach the results. At the top, six selection panels are presented in a carousel that shows two panels at a time. Use the ‹ › arrows, the step pills in the left column, or swipe on mobile to navigate three pages.

    Below the carousel, personalized AI tools and ideas for activities appear instantly. A chip bar between the zones shows your current selections at a glance.

    You only need two selections to start — the more you add, the more precise the matches.

    2 Your Left-Hand Column

    One Column, Three Jobs — It Follows You Down the Page

    The column beneath the Library logo is sticky: it stays with you as you scroll, and it swaps its contents to match what you are doing.

    While you are choosing, it holds the six numbered step pills. Click any one to jump straight to that panel.

    Once the panels scroll away, the pills give way to Results actions — Regenerate, Copy Link, Export PDF, Lower-stakes ideas, and Reset All — plus ↑ Back to selections to return to the top.

    5 Explore Results

    Tools, Activity Ideas & Category Badges

    Results appear below the carousel. Recommended AI Tools shows top-ranked tools with links, access badges, and short disciplinary descriptions. Click any tool name to visit its site directly.

    Ideas for Activities displays time estimates, Bloom's levels, discipline tags, and category badges — hover the filter buttons (All ideas, Lower-stakes ideas, Teaching/course ideas, Faculty workflow ideas) for a quick explanation of each.

    Click any activity-idea card to open the adaptation notes, provenance, considerations, and related readings. Recommendations come from a transparent weighted rules engine, and the provenance labels show whether a result is direct, synthesized, framework-based, or an original design (HCAI-assisted).
